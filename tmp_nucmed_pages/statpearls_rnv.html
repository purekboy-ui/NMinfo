Radionuclide Ventriculography(Archived)

Vijaykumar Bodar; Kalgi Modi.

Author Information and Affiliations

Authors

Vijaykumar Bodar1; Kalgi Modi2.

Affiliations

1 Louisiana State University, Shreveport

Last Update: August 14, 2023.

Introduction

The radionuclide ventriculography (RNV) scan is a noninvasive method for assessing ventricular function and intracardiac hemodynamics. The lower inter-observer variability of the RNV scan makes it a preferred method for serial monitoring of ventricular function.[1] It also assesses regional wall contractility, which may determine myocardial synchrony before cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT).[2][3] Furthermore, it is a safe and relatively quick procedure that can be performed in 1 to 2 hours. It is performed by injecting a radioactive tracer and acquiring images of the cardiac chambers with a gamma camera. The radioactive tracer outlines the ventricular cavity precisely, enabling accurate assessment of ventricular function.[4] The RNV scan can be performed using 3 approaches: first-pass radionuclide ventriculography, equilibrium blood-pool ventriculography, and gated single-photon emission computed tomography (Gated SPECT).[4]

Anatomy and Physiology

The RNV scan can help examine the anatomy and physiology of the cardiac cycle. The radionuclide tracer is based on the principle that the amount of radioactivity in each chamber is proportional to the volume of that chamber.[5] The volume of each ventricle at the end of systole is called end-systolic volume (ESV), and at the end of diastole is called end-diastolic volume (EDV). The difference between EDV and ESV represents stroke volume (normal range: 80-100 mL). While the ejection fraction (EF) is the percentage of the EDV ejected by the ventricle during systole, cardiac output is the product of the stroke volume and the heart rate (HR).

Wall motion can be assessed on RNV scans and classified as normal, hypokinetic (decreased wall motion), akinetic (no wall motion), or dyskinetic (paradoxical wall motion). The heart muscle receives its vascular supply through the coronary arteries; the left anterior descending coronary artery supplies the anterior wall, intraventricular septum, apex, and a part of the anterolateral wall of the left ventricle; the left circumflex artery supplies the left atrium, posterolateral wall and a part of the anterolateral wall; and the right coronary artery supplies right ventricle, right atrium and the posterior portion of the intraventricular septum. By delineating segmental wall-motion abnormalities associated with ischemia or infarction, radionuclide ventriculography can help localize the coronary artery supplying the affected myocardium.

Indications

Indications of radionuclide ventriculography include: 

  • Assessment of the left/right ventricular systolic function in a patient with coronary artery disease (CAD), valvular heart disease, atrial arrhythmia, congenital heart disease, myocardial disease, or before starting cardiotoxic chemotherapy.
  • Assessment of left ventricular diastolic function in patients with congestive heart failure (CHF) with preserved EF
  • Serial monitoring of the left ventricular function while on cardiotoxic chemotherapy
  • Regional wall motion assessment in a patient with the low-intermediate probability of CAD when myocardial stress perfusion imaging (MPI) is not available. 
  • Assessment of the ventricular synchronicity prior to CRT placement

Contraindications

Contraindications include:

  • In pregnant or nursing patients, to avoid radiation exposure to the fetus or baby
  • History of an allergic reaction to the radioactive tracer

Equipment

Equipment needed includes:

  • Electrocardiogram machine
  • Blood pressure machine
  • A radioactive tracer (TI-201 or Tc-99m-labeled)
  • Gamma camera
  • Treadmill or stationary bicycle for exercise scan 
  • Computer system with software for final data processing and analysis

Personnel

The RNV scan should be performed by a qualified nuclear medicine technologist, with image acquisition and processing performed under the supervision of a cardiologist, radiologist, or nuclear medicine physician.  

Preparation

The remainder of the RNV scan does not require any specific preparation. The exercise RNV scan would require 4 to 6 hours of fasting. Additionally, the patient should avoid caffeinated beverages for 12 hours before the procedure and wear comfortable clothing and shoes. 

Technique or Treatment

The patient is comfortably positioned on the examination table; EKG (electrocardiogram) leads are attached to the chest wall, and a blood pressure cuff is wrapped around the arm. Baseline heart rate, EKG, and blood pressure are recorded before the test and then monitored closely throughout the exam.

The scan requires tagging the patient's red blood cells (RBCs) with the radiotracer (99mTc), which can be performed in vivo or in vitro, and then injecting the radiotracer (approximately 20 mCi per patient weight) via an intravenous line. Subsequently, the study can proceed using one of three methods: first-pass study, gated equilibrium ventriculography, or gated SPECT image acquisition. EKG gating is achieved by dividing the cardiac cycle into 16 or 32 frames and using the R wave as a trigger to ensure proper sampling. One prerequisite for EKG gating is a baseline premature ventricular contraction rate of less than 10%. A variation in heart rate or rhythm, such as sinus arrhythmia, distorts the data. The exercise scan can be performed using a stationary bicycle or a pharmacological agent (e.g., dobutamine). 

In the first-pass study, the gamma camera acquires images of the heart, lungs, and great vessels in rapid succession during the bolus's initial rapid transit, with a 30-degree RAO projection. 

In gated equilibrium radionuclide ventriculography, images are acquired after the radiotracer has reached equilibrium. It acquires images in three views: an anterior view, a left oblique view (45 degrees) to visualize the septum, and a left lateral oblique view.  During exercise, only the left anterior oblique view is obtained at different stages of exercise. 

In a gated SPECT acquisition, the structure of interest can be precisely isolated to accurately assess ventricular segment contractility. It produces long-axis, short-axis, and three-dimensional volumetric images. The ejection fraction assessed with the SPECT images is more accurate than the planar method due to atrial interference in the planar method, which would underestimate the ejection fraction. 

Qualitative and Quantitative Assessment 

To analyze the data qualitatively and quantitatively, different images, including functional, stroke volume, paradox, and amplitude images, are obtained. In the qualitative assessment, the cinematic display on the screen enables visual evaluation of ventricular contractility and wall-motion abnormalities. In quantitative analysis, the computer counts the change in radioactivity within the region of interest (ROI) over a cardiac cycle, which is proportional to the change in blood volume in that area.  From this, the time-activity curve can be generated to obtain the end-systolic and end-diastolic volumes.  The accuracy of the calculation depends on the precise selection of the region of interest. Additionally, regional ejection fraction and contractility are assessed by segmenting the ventricle.

Complications

The RNV scan is a safe procedure with no serious adverse reactions, as it uses a small dose of the radiotracer, which is cleared by the kidneys within 24 hours. The radiation exposure from RNV is equivalent to that of a computed tomography (CT) scan. Dobutamine used during the exercise scan may cause nausea, headache, palpitations, or chest discomfort, which usually resolves within a few minutes.

Clinical Significance

The RNV scan is a gold-standard test for assessing left ventricular function before and during cardiotoxic chemotherapy. Additionally, it has proven to be a cost-effective method for monitoring ventricular function during cardiotoxic chemotherapy.[6] The previous study has reported its usefulness in assessing perioperative cardiac risk and mortality.[7] 

The regional wall motion abnormality reported on the scan could signal towards coronary ischemia or infarction. Additionally, it can provide prognostic information regarding coronary artery disease and acute coronary events.[8][9] Furthermore, it can aid in the differential diagnosis of dilated, hypertrophic, or restrictive cardiomyopathies. Ventricular synchrony assessed by radionuclide ventriculography may be helpful prior to CRT, as echocardiographic parameters have not consistently predicted cardiac outcomes.[3][10]

Enhancing Healthcare Team Outcomes

The primary care provider can use the RNV scan to assess left ventricular function in patients with new signs or symptoms of CHF, particularly those receiving cardiotoxic chemotherapy. The test can be performed in 1 to 2 hours, with results obtained immediately. Patient care should be coordinated with cardiology and oncology whenever ventricular function declines to adjust the chemotherapy regimen. 

Nursing, Allied Health, and Interprofessional Team Interventions

The team coordinator should call the patient to confirm the date, time, and location of the procedure. The nursing staff can contact the patient a few days prior to the appointment to discuss test-day preparation and expectations.  On a test day, the technician should monitor the time and protocol. Additionally, the technician should instruct the patient appropriately to lessen the motion and obtain accurate images. 

Nursing, Allied Health, and Interprofessional Team Monitoring

The patient should undergo continuous EKG monitoring during the test. The nursing staff should observe the patient for any allergic reaction to the radioactive tracer and notify the supervising physician immediately. The patient should be monitored with serial blood pressure readings and continuous ECG rhythm during the exercise scan. The facility should be equipped with an emergency response system for a pharmacological exercise scan.
